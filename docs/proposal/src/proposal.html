Robust and acceptable pathways to low-emission regional mobility: coupling estimated travel behaviour, agent-based simulation and backcasting under deep uncertainty

1Background

1.1 Policy context

Norway’s National Transport Plan 2022–2033 aims to halve greenhouse-gas emissions from transport by 2030 relative to 2005 and to bring the sector close to zero emissions by 20501. The obstacle is less ambition than inertia. In 2025, 95.9% of new passenger cars sold in Norway were fully electric11, yet only about one car in three on the road is; with cars typically kept for close to two decades, much of the fleet that will still be driving in 2035 has already been bought12 (Figure 1). Road-transport emissions in 2020 were still above their 1990 level13. Whether the targets are met depends largely on household decisions — how people travel and which vehicles they buy — which respond to policy, prices and technology in ways that are only partly known. The Norwegian Centre for Sustainable Transport Planning (TRANSPLAN, 2025–2033) takes the 2050 low-emission society as a premise and asks which transport system, models and policy instruments are consistent with it2. This project contributes to that agenda for a low-density fjord region, using Molde as the main case and Kristiansund as a transfer case.

1.2 Why a single forecast is not enough

Strategic transport planning relies on demand models that produce one forecast for one set of assumptions. The record is sobering: across 210 projects in 14 nations, rail passenger forecasts were overestimated in nine cases out of ten, by 106% on average; half of road forecasts missed by more than ±20%; and accuracy did not improve over thirty years3. When the main drivers of 2050 emissions — electric-vehicle prices, fuel prices, telework, population growth, social influence on vehicle choice, and how well behavioural parameters transfer to new conditions — cannot be given reliable probabilities, the planning problem is one of deep uncertainty4. This project does not replace the national and regional models (NTM6, RTM), which remain the best single estimate; it builds the exploratory layer around them and asks which decisions still hold when that estimate turns out wrong. The question then changes from “what will happen?” to “which policies perform acceptably across the futures that could happen, and when must they be adopted?”4,5 Figure 2 shows this decision problem as one chain, from a single commuting trip to a 2050 verdict, exactly as it runs in the project’s working prototype.

In the prototype, no policy reaches the target in only 4 of 60 futures. The most robust of 25 packages reaches it in 41 — but only with a NOK 54 toll per car trip, near-exemption of electric cars from that toll, nearly halved bus headways and a NOK 99,000 electric-vehicle purchase subsidy. A package can win on emissions and still fail with the public or the budget. That tension is the subject of this project.

2Problem statement and research gap

Two modelling traditions each address part of the problem. Discrete choice models estimate how travellers trade off time, cost and service attributes, with well-understood statistical properties6, but they describe behaviour at one point in time. Agent-based models represent dynamics — the spread of electric vehicles through social networks, congestion feedback, fleet turnover — but their behavioural rules are often assumed rather than estimated. Coupling the two has been demonstrated for the ex-ante evaluation of urban freight policies, where choice models estimated from stakeholder data drive an agent-based simulation of policy acceptability7,8. Separately, exploratory modelling and scenario discovery search for policies that are robust across many futures4,5,9, and adaptive pathways sequence decisions over time10.

Three recent strands come close. Backcasting has been formulated as optimal control of a single trajectory — for example, scheduling electric-vehicle incentives for France from 2022 to 205014. Large agent-based models have simulated 2050 under a handful of scenario bundles15. And two decades of survey research show that acceptance of road pricing falls with the charge level, rises when revenues are earmarked or instruments are bundled, and can rise once a scheme has been experienced16,17. Yet the optimal trajectory is optimal for one assumed future, the scenario bundles are few and fixed in advance, and acceptability is measured after policies are designed rather than used to design them. A search of the 2023–2026 literature found no study that couples estimated choice models, an agent-based model and deep-uncertainty exploration, and none that treats acceptability as a formal condition for robustness.

What is missing is a framework that brings these together for long-range passenger policy: estimated behaviour, with its uncertainty, driving an agent model; the coupled model explored across thousands of futures; acceptability and cost treated as conditions for robustness rather than separate assessments; and the result sequenced backwards from a 2050 target into timed decisions. Figure 3 locates this gap.

what is new
  1. Acceptability as a condition for robustness. A package counts as robust only if most affected households would accept it — using acceptance estimated from the region’s own survey data, not assumed.
  2. Estimation uncertainty carried into the futures. Preference parameters are drawn from their estimated distribution in every future, so whether behaviour transfers to 2050 is explored rather than fixed.
  3. Backcasting under deep uncertainty. Milestones, signposts and trigger points are read from thousands of futures instead of one optimal trajectory.

3Aim, objectives and research questions

Aim. To develop and demonstrate a reproducible framework that identifies policy packages which reach a 2050 emission target across most plausible futures while remaining acceptable to the people affected and affordable, and to sequence them into a timed pathway for a Norwegian region.

Definition of robustness used throughout. For a package p evaluated over a set of futures F:

R(p) = share of futures f ∈ F with E2050(p, f) ≤ τ · E2025 τ = 0.2 in the prototype
p* = argmax R(p) subject to A(p) ≥ ½ and C(p) ≤ B
A(p): share of the population accepting p (estimated in A1) · C(p): public cost of p · B: budget

Objectives.

  • O1 — Estimate mode, vehicle-technology and policy-acceptance preferences for the region from a stated-choice survey and travel-survey data.
  • O2 — Couple the estimated models into an agent-based model, calibrated and validated against observed shares and fleet data.
  • O3 — Explore the coupled model over thousands of futures and search for admissible robust packages and their failure conditions.
  • O4 — Backcast from the 2050 target to milestones, signposts and trigger points, and test how they are best communicated to planners.

Research questions.

  1. RQ1How do households in a low-density fjord region trade off travel time, cost and vehicle technology; how heterogeneous and transferable are these preferences; and which policy packages would they accept?
  2. RQ2How can estimated choice and acceptance models drive an agent-based model so that diffusion and network feedback remain behaviourally consistent, and how should the coupled model be calibrated and validated?
  3. RQ3Across thousands of futures, which admissible packages maximise robustness (or minimise regret), which combinations of uncertainties make them fail, and does the same procedure carry over to freight?
  4. RQ4Starting from the 2050 target, what must hold by 2030 and 2035, which signposts indicate that a pathway is failing, and do visual representations of pathways help stakeholders reach agreement?

Each question is answered by one article, and each article hands a defined product to the next (Figure 4).

4The case: two fjord towns

A road network is a set of answers to the question “where else could I go?”. In Molde and Kristiansund the fjord answers it first.

4.1 Why Molde, and why Kristiansund

Molde and Kristiansund are the two regional centres of northern Møre og Romsdal. They are of similar size, car-dependent, and have no rail. Their geography is a natural experiment in network fragility. Molde lies on a narrow shelf between fjord and mountain: routing a vehicle between every pair of its 2,772 road junctions, 17.5% of all fastest routes use one street, Øvre veg. Kristiansund is built on islands joined by bridges: a quarter of all fastest routes (24.9%) cross Gomabrua (Figure 5). In places like these, a toll cordon, a bus priority lane or a closed bridge changes travel for most of the town at once. A model that ignores the network will misjudge both the effect of a policy and who is affected by it.

Molde is the main case: choice models are estimated there and the agent model is calibrated there. Kristiansund is the transfer case: the Molde model is applied without re-estimation and checked against observed shares. This gives an empirical value for the transferability uncertainty that the ensemble otherwise has to assume (the cost- and time-sensitivity scales in Figure 2d). The corridor between the towns is left to future work, because no consistent network extract for it exists yet.

The survey size comes from a recovery test rather than a rule of thumb. With 800 simulated respondents answering eight tasks each, all six parameters of the prototype’s choice model were recovered within one standard error of their true values, and the value of time within 2% (NOK 142 against 144 per hour). The real survey adds a mixed-logit layer, so the target is set at this size or larger.

Evidence already produced on the case. Four explainer plates run on the same Molde network (Figure 6). They establish that the method works on real geography before any survey money is spent. They also open the logistics side of the project: urban freight shares the same pinch points.

5Methods: four articles, one model chain

Each article answers one research question, can be published on its own, and hands a defined product to the next.

A1 · RQ1 · Year 1

Preferences and acceptance

Data Stated-choice survey in Molde and Kristiansund with three blocks of tasks: mode choice, vehicle technology, and votes on policy packages (charge level, exemptions, how revenue is used). RVU trips for revealed-preference anchoring. Method Multinomial, mixed and latent-class logit6, with willingness to pay and value of time by segment. Package acceptance is modelled as a binary or ordered choice whose attributes are the levers of Figure 2c, following the evidence that acceptance falls with charge level and rises with earmarking and bundling16,17. Transfer test Estimate on Molde, predict Kristiansund, compare. Product Estimated utilities with their covariance matrix, and an acceptance function A(p). Venue Transportation Research Part A or Transport Policy.

A2 · RQ2 · Year 2

The coupled agent model

Method Agents choose mode and vehicle with the A1 utilities. Parameters are drawn from their estimated distribution, not fixed at point estimates. EV adoption spreads through neighbourhood peer effects, and car travel times respond to congestion on the OSM network. Agents vote with A(p), so acceptance becomes an outcome of the simulation. Calibration and validation Pattern-oriented modelling18: mode shares (RVU), fleet composition (SSB) and traffic counts must be matched at the same time, with Kristiansund as a hold-out. Documented with the ODD protocol19. Product A calibrated model and an ensemble interface. Venue Transportation Research Part C or Environmental Modelling & Software.

A3 · RQ3 · Year 3

Robust and admissible packages

Method Around 104 futures × 102 packages, with the futures sampled by Latin hypercube5. Each package is scored by satisficing robustness R(p) subject to A(p) ≥ ½ and C(p) ≤ B, with regret and alternative robustness metrics reported for comparison20. PRIM scenario discovery finds the failure conditions9, and Sobol indices rank the uncertainties21. If the model is too slow for the ensemble, a statistical surrogate is trained on a subset of runs. Freight The same procedure is applied to urban delivery on the Molde network using the routing module (Figure 6, plates 01–04). Product Admissible robust packages, their failure boxes, and a data tree indexed {future; package; year}. Venue Transportation Research Part D or Technological Forecasting & Social Change.

A4 · RQ4 · Year 3

From 2050 back to now

Method Backcasting from the 2050 target to milestones, read from the successful trajectories of the ensemble. Signposts and triggers22 are chosen by how well an indicator observable in 2030 separates futures that go on to succeed from those that fail. These are assembled into adaptive pathways10, including switches to instruments outside the original lever set. Workshop Planners from the county, the two municipalities and TRANSPLAN partners compare a pathway map with a conventional table of results, as a within-subject test of whether the visual form helps them agree on the next decision. Venue Futures or Environmental Modelling & Software.

6What the prototype already shows

The behaviour is synthetic and the numbers below are not findings about Norway. They show what each article will produce, and that the chain from Figure 2 runs end to end.

6.1 Policy changes the odds, not the future

Without policy, commuting emissions reach the 2050 target in 4 of 60 futures. With package P12, they reach it in 41. Even with P12, emissions in 2050 still range from 9% to 30% of the 2025 level across the futures; without policy they range from 14% to 50% (Figure 8). No package removes the uncertainty. The best one makes success likely and makes failure recognisable early.

6.2 The price of robustness

Robustness has a cost, and the ensemble can price it. P12 combines a NOK 54 toll per car trip, near-exemption of electric cars from that toll, nearly halved bus headways, bike infrastructure and a NOK 99,000 EV purchase subsidy. Measured as public money moved per commuter per year, it pays out about NOK 5,390 in subsidies and collects NOK 3,810 in tolls, a net cost of about NOK 1,580. The best package that pays for itself, P21, reaches the target in 37 futures instead of 41 and raises a net NOK 2,900 per commuter per year (Figure 9). The trade-off between four futures and NOK 4,480 per commuter per year is a political choice. Making it explicit is the purpose of the budget constraint C(p) ≤ B.

Acceptance in the prototype is only a proxy: the share of commuters whose commuting is not made worse in 2030. By that measure, 21 of 24 packages, P12 included, are acceptable to a majority. This is a lower bound from self-interest alone and is very likely optimistic. In 2019, a party founded to oppose road tolls won 16.7% of the vote in Bergen’s municipal election23. This is why A1 estimates acceptance directly instead of inferring it from travel times.

6.3 Where every package fails, and how early we would know

The 19 futures in which no package succeeds share a signature. An exhaustive search for the densest two-threshold box finds that cars kept for 12.9 years or more, combined with 2050 telework at or below 12.7% of commute days, describes 16 futures, of which 14 fail every package (Figure 10a). This is the inertia of Figure 1 reappearing inside the model: where cars are replaced slowly and commuting does not shrink, levers that act on new purchases and on trip costs cannot reach the target in time. With 60 futures the box is a hypothesis, to be re-tested on 104 futures in A3.

The failure is also visible early. If commuting CO₂ in 2030 is still above 63% of its 2025 level, the pathway is off track. In the prototype this single reading identifies 17 of the 19 failing futures, with 4 false alarms among the 41 successes (Figure 10b), twenty years before the target year. A signpost with a measured hit rate is what turns a backcast into a plan.

6.4 The pathway

Put together, the prototype already yields the skeleton of A4’s product. A package starts in 2026 and is at full strength by 2030. Milestones of 58%, 36% and 26% of 2025 emissions are set for 2030, 2035 and 2040. One signpost is read in 2030. If the signpost fails, the evidence of Figure 10a points beyond the five levers, to instruments that speed up fleet renewal or reduce commuting (Figure 11). Designing and testing those switches is the work of A4.

what the prototype does not show
  1. Real preferences: all behaviour is synthetic until the A1 survey.
  2. Network routing: agents have trip lengths, not routes. The congestion model is zonal.
  3. Freight: plates 01–04 run on the real network but are not yet coupled to the ensemble.
  4. Statistical confidence in the boxes and signposts: 60 futures are a demonstration, not an analysis.

7Work plan, risks and ethics

The prototype is Year 0. Each later year replaces one synthetic layer with an observed one, and ends with an article.

RiskMitigation
Low survey responsePanel recruitment plus municipal channels. The size target is set by a recovery test, and RVU anchors the revealed-preference side.
RVU access delayedSSB commuting flows and the survey’s own revealed-preference questions cover calibration until access is granted.
Agent model too slow for 106 runsAbout a hundred core-hours at present speed. If that grows, use a statistical surrogate, national HPC (Sigma2) or fewer packages after screening.
Calibration not identifiablePattern-oriented calibration against several observed patterns at once, with Kristiansund as a hold-out.
Results hinge on the target or the robustness metricReport results for targets of 10%, 20% and 30% of 2025, and compare satisficing, regret and other metrics20.
Stakeholder workshopRecruited through TRANSPLAN and the county from Year 2. A remote format as fallback.

Ethics and data management. The survey collects personal data. It will be assessed by Sikt before fieldwork, with informed consent, pseudonymised storage at Molde University College, and deletion of identifiers after linkage. Code is open under the MIT licence. Model inputs, ensemble outputs and anonymised survey data will be deposited with a DOI when consent allows, following the FAIR principles. An anti-drift register in the repository will record every change to targets, levers and assumptions, so that the published results can be traced to the version that produced them.

8Expected contributions

  • To transport science: a framework in which acceptability is a condition for robustness rather than a separate check; estimation uncertainty carried from the survey into deep-uncertainty exploration of an agent model; and backcasting with signposts whose hit rates are measured on the ensemble.
  • To TRANSPLAN: an exploratory layer for Research Area 1 that works alongside the national and regional models (NTM6, RTM). It asks which strategies remain sound when their forecasts are wrong, in a region type the national models resolve coarsely.
  • To the region: a tested pathway for Molde and Kristiansund with indicators planners can monitor using data they already collect (traffic counts, the vehicle register).
  • To logistics: the same robustness procedure applied to urban freight on the same network, supported by the routing and network tools already built.
  • To practice: an open, tested toolchain and a visual language for showing uncertainty to the people who must decide under it.

The fleet that will drive in 2035 is being bought now. The decisions that steer it must be made before the evidence is in. This project builds the instruments to make those decisions well, and to know early when they need to change.
