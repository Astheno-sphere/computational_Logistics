phd proposal / layer one — the spine

Robust pathways to low-emission mobility

Which transport policies still reach Norway’s 2050 targets when the future refuses to cooperate — and how should a realistic plan sequence them?

Coupling estimated choice behaviour · agent-based simulation · exploratory modelling · backcasting Case Molde, with Kristiansund as transfer test Context PhD in Logistics, Molde University College · TRANSPLAN
LAYER 1 · ≈450 WORDS · 1 OF 5 PAGES
01 — THE PROBLEM

One forecast,
many futures

Norway’s National Transport Plan 2022–2033 aims to halve transport emissions by 2030 against 2005 and to approach zero by 2050. The plan must work in a future nobody can forecast: battery and fuel prices, telework, population, and whether people accept the measures are all deeply uncertain.

Point forecasts are the wrong instrument for that. Traffic forecasts for major projects have long been shown to miss systematically, and a plan tuned to one expected future can fail quietly in most of the others. TRANSPLAN starts from the other end: assume the 2050 goals are met, then ask what must be true for that to hold.

The research need is not a better forecast. It is a way to find policies that keep working when the forecast is wrong.

Ministry of Transport, National Transport Plan 2022–2033 (Meld. St. 20 2020–2021). Flyvbjerg, Skamris Holm & Buhl, Transport Reviews 26(1), 2006. Lempert, Popper & Bankes, Shaping the Next One Hundred Years, RAND, 2003.
02 — THE GAP

Two traditions,
one seam

Discrete choice models estimate how people trade time, cost and technology with real statistical rigour, but at one point in time. Agent-based models capture what happens over time — diffusion through neighbours, congestion feedback — but often rest on behaviour that was assumed rather than estimated.

The two have been coupled before, and at Molde: Le Pira, Marcucci, Gatta and colleagues used choice models inside an agent simulation to rank urban-freight policies by stakeholder acceptability. That coupling has not yet been carried into long-range passenger pathways, explored across thousands of futures and backcasted from a 2050 target.

That is the seam this PhD works in: estimated behaviour driving agents, the coupled model explored under deep uncertainty, and the result sequenced backwards into a plan.

Le Pira, Marcucci, Gatta, Inturri, Ignaccolo & Pluchino, Research in Transportation Economics 64, 2017. Marcucci, Le Pira, Gatta et al., Transportation Research Part E 103, 2017. Kwakkel, Environmental Modelling & Software 96, 2017 (EMA Workbench).
03 — THE AIM

Robust,
defined

The aim is a reproducible framework that finds and sequences policy packages for a Norwegian region that reach a 2050 emissions target across most plausible futures. “Robust” is defined once and used throughout:

A package is robust if it reaches the target in the largest share of futures, while being accepted by a majority of the people it affects and staying within a stated budget.

One objective, two constraints. Acceptance and cost are not afterthoughts added to a ranking; they decide which packages are allowed to compete at all. The same survey that estimates behaviour also measures acceptance, so both come from one instrument.

04 — THE QUESTIONS

Four questions,
one loop

  1. RQ1Behaviour. How do households in a low-density fjord region trade off time, cost and vehicle technology, how heterogeneous and transferable are those preferences, and which packages would they accept? → A1
  2. RQ2Coupling. How can estimated choice and acceptance models drive an agent-based model so that diffusion and network feedback stay behaviourally consistent and calibrated? → A2
  3. RQ3Exploration. Across thousands of futures, which packages minimise regret under the acceptance and budget constraints, which conditions make them fail, and does the same engine carry over to freight? → A3
  4. RQ4Backcasting. From the 2050 target, what must hold by 2030 and 2035, which signposts show the plan drifting, and do visual pathways help stakeholders agree on it? → A4
