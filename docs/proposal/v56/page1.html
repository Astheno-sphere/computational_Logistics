01 · THE PROBLEM

What an equilibrium cannot carry

Norway appraises transport with a national model system that computes an equilibrium for a chosen year. Asked what 2050 looks like under a set of assumptions, it answers carefully. Asked how the country gets there, and which routes fail on the way, it is silent by construction. TØI’s own review says as much: the current models are static and macroscopic, and less suited to dynamic questions [toi1819]. The path to 2050 is governed by what an equilibrium does not carry: a vehicle fleet that turns over slowly, public acceptance that persists or erodes, and debt repaid on a schedule.

Road pricing shows the problem in its sharpest form. It is named among the levers for the next National Transport Plan, where TRANSPLAN’s first deliverable argues the plan must be realistic and goal-directed rather than a wish list [toi2172]; yet the national reform has stalled. Tolls raised NOK 16.8 billion in 2025, about 62% in city packages, against toll-company debt near NOK 78 billion [svv25]. Every revision meets a public that has voted against tolls before: a single-issue anti-toll party won 16.7% in Bergen in 2019 [snl19]. Whether a 2050 plan is feasible depends on the order in which instruments arrive and whether people accept each step. That is a question about paths.

Main question. Which sequences of road-pricing instruments reach a desirable 2050 on emissions, finance and accessibility while staying acceptable to the people and firms who pay, across deep uncertainty; and which signals tell planners when to switch?
RQ1 · methodHow can choice models estimated on real data drive agents that carry state year to year, so strategic end states become pathways?
RQ2 · behaviourDoes acceptance persist once a charge is permanent, and does information move it independently of experience?
RQ3 · planningWhich pathways, for passengers and for freight, are robust; what acceptance margin does each survive; and which signposts give lead time before one fails?

What already exists. I have a working prototype and a first measured result. At the Kristiansund tariff a zero-emission car with a tag pays NOK 19.04 against NOK 27.20, so each electric crossing earns NOK 8.16, or 30%, less [vegamot]. On the road network, the toll-free Atlantic Ocean Tunnel lets some crossings avoid both stations; Article 1 counts that share ([N1]), so exposure is measured on the network, not assumed uniform. The question is no longer whether the toll base erodes; it is which path through that erosion people will accept.
